CHIMERA-Agent

In progress

Monash AIM · Architecture and build

A retrieval-grounded risk-stratification agent over MRI and biopsy data. The architecture is deliberately hybrid: a deterministic guideline core carries the clinical logic, and the LLM is constrained to what it can actually support from the retrieved patient record.

The design decision I care most about is that risk-group derivation retrieves against published clinical guidelines and returns candidate categories with provenance traces rather than hard labels. Every output can be walked back to the source that produced it. In a clinical setting that is the difference between something deployable and something you can only demo.

CohortX

Delivered

Monash AIM · Team Lead, four people, three tasks

An LLM pipeline that reads biomedical literature and pulls out the cohort selection criteria a trial actually used — free text in, structured eligibility criteria out. I led the team and personally delivered two of the three tasks end to end: parsing, prompting, eligibility prediction, evaluation and the submission pipeline.

Most of the engineering was not the model. It was deciding which model was worth the compute we had, and building something that could tell us whether a change had helped.

  • Evaluated open-weight models for the extraction task — LLaMA 3.1 8B as the primary, with Mistral Nemo and BioBERT as fallbacks — against cost and the compute we could actually get.
  • Built a local evaluation harness implementing the challenge's FM3S metric: BioBERT cosine similarity, number similarity and a polarity consistency coefficient.
  • Caught two faults that were failing silently: a mismatch between parsed and expected file counts, and a scorer whose composite weights I couldn't verify — which meant leaderboard feedback was the only signal worth trusting.
  • Restructured ownership and handover mid-project so the team kept delivering to deadline through a period I was away.

The team's third-task result earned a contribution to a MICCAI paper and presentation, currently in preparation.

NeuroGrip

In progress

Monash DeepNeuron · AI Lead on a three-person build

The project goal is a prosthetic arm controlled autonomously by AI. I own the computer vision pipeline: detecting the target object and driving finger movement from what the camera sees, alongside a mechanical lead and an electrical lead. I take over the AI team next semester as the project scales, owning architecture and scope. It runs on the device — monocular depth from Depth Anything with an optional stereo path, deployed to an NVIDIA Jetson NEON smart camera.

What it does in practice: track a taught fingertip and the target object, estimate the gap between them in millimetres once calibrated, and hand the electrical controller a movement direction. Around that sits the unglamorous half — camera calibration for both the single and stereo paths, a shared-memory and Ethernet bus between processes, synthetic scene generation so the pipeline could be tested before any hardware existed, and a telemetry dashboard.

The rule I built in from the start: actuation stays disabled until calibration and validation pass. The software will not send a movement command to a physical hand it has not been proven against. A grip system that is confidently wrong breaks something or someone, and the interlock is cheaper than finding out.

NeuroBOLT

In progress

Monash, FIT2082 · Supervised by a PhD researcher and an AI academic

A research project assessing whether transformer models can predict fMRI brain activity from EEG recordings, worked on a large paired neuroimaging dataset. I rebuilt the NeurIPS 2024 NeuroBOLT architecture from scratch — learning across temporal, spatial and spectral representations, on an existing foundation model with multi-scale spectral fusion.

Reimplementing rather than running the authors' code is the point: you cannot hand-wave a component you have to write. The conclusion was that it is a promising direction, and the work is continuing.

How I work

I don't trust a number I can't reproduce

Most of what went wrong on CohortX went wrong quietly — files that vanished between stages, a scorer that looked fine and wasn't. The habit I took from it is checking the signal before trusting the result it gives you.

Delegation that survives me leaving

A team that only works while one person watches it isn't a team. The test I care about is whether the work keeps moving when I'm not there — and on CohortX it did.

Constrain the model, don't trust it

The clinical work pushed me toward architectures where the LLM is the smallest part that can do the job and everything around it is deterministic and inspectable. Ungrounded output is fine in a demo and useless anywhere it matters.

I'd rather say what I don't know yet

There are things I haven't done — I've run training on HPC rather than on AWS or GCP, for instance. I'd rather be clear about that than be caught out at the point it matters. I pick things up quickly under a real deadline and I finish what I take on.
